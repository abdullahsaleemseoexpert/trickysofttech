What are local citations, and do they still matter?

A local citation is any online mention of your business that includes its name, address, and phone number, usually in a business directory, a map or app, or a data aggregator. Google and other engines use these mentions to cross-check that your business is real and that your details agree across the web.

Citations are not the strongest local ranking factor. Your Business Profile, reviews, and website usually carry more weight. But they are the base the rest sits on. Consistent listings help engines trust your details, they put you in front of people who search directories directly, and a few of them, such as trade platforms, send real customers. Wrong or conflicting listings do the opposite, so it pays to get them right once. Our guide on what local citations are and whether they still matter goes deeper.

The right order to build citations

  1. Core platforms. Google Business Profile, Apple Business, Bing Places, and Facebook. These feed maps, search, and voice results directly.
  2. Data aggregators. Sites such as Foursquare, and in the US Data Axle and Neustar Localeze, pass your details to many other directories.
  3. The strongest general directories for your country, such as Yelp, Yellow Pages, or Yell.
  4. Niche directories for your trade, such as a legal, medical, home services, or restaurant platform. These are often where customers actually look.
  5. Local sources. Your chamber of commerce, local business associations, and regional directories. These are not all in this list, so search your own area.

Going in this order means the high-trust sources are correct first, and any data that flows downstream starts out clean.

How to keep NAP consistent across every listing

  • Write your NAP once and paste it, rather than retyping it. Keep it in a document or spreadsheet.
  • Match your Business Profile. Use the same business name, address format, and primary phone number as your Google listing and your website.
  • Be careful with abbreviations. Choose “Street” or “St.” and stick with it. Do not add keywords to the business name.
  • Use one main phone number. Tracking numbers are fine if you handle them carefully, but your main number should be the one on the core listings.
  • Record every login. Note the site, the email used, and the date, so you can update or remove a listing later.
  • Update everywhere when you move or rebrand. Treat it as a project, starting with the core platforms.

For the full method, read our guide on NAP consistency and local rankings.

Cleaning up duplicate and wrong listings

  1. Search for your business by name, by phone number, and by old addresses, to find listings you did not know about.
  2. Claim before you delete. If a directory has a listing for you, claim it and correct it. Claiming also stops it being changed again.
  3. Request removal of true duplicates. Most directories have a way to report a duplicate or ask for a listing to be merged or removed.
  4. Start with the sites that matter most. Fix the core platforms and aggregators first, then work outward.
  5. Allow time. Corrections can take weeks to spread, and some old data will linger. Recheck after a month.

Frequently asked questions

How many citations do I need?

There is no magic number. Start with the core platforms and the data aggregators, then add the strongest general directories for your country, then the niche sites that fit your trade. For most local businesses, 30 to 50 accurate listings on relevant, real directories covers the ground. After that, quality, consistency, and reviews matter more than adding listing number 80.

Are paid directories worth it?

Sometimes. Pay when the directory sends real customers in your niche, such as a trade marketplace or a medical booking platform, and you can track leads from it. Do not pay only for the citation. A free listing on a general directory gives you the same consistency benefit, so judge a paid listing on the leads it can bring, not on the link.

How long do citations take to show up?

It varies. Some directories publish a new listing within minutes or days, while others take several weeks to review it. Data aggregators can take longer to pass your details on to the sites they feed. Keep your tracker up to date and check again after a month before you assume a listing failed.

Do citations help service-area businesses?

Yes, with care. A service-area business that has hidden its address should use the same approach on every listing, showing the service area where the site allows it and never publishing a fake storefront address. Consistency of name, phone, and website matters just as much, and the directories that serve your trade are often the most useful.

Should I use a citation service?

A service can save time, but check what you are getting. The risks are listings created with wrong details, accounts you do not control, and duplicates that are hard to remove later. If you use one, make sure the accounts end up in your name, you approve every listing, and you can see where each one went. Doing the core platforms yourself is a good idea either way.

What is the difference between structured and unstructured citations?

A structured citation is a listing in a directory with set fields for name, address, phone, and category. An unstructured citation is a mention of your business details in other content, such as a news story, blog post, or local event page. Both can help, but structured listings are what this tool tracks, because you can create and control them.
