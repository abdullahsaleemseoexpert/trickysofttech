How to Report Fake Google Business Profile Listings and Map Spam

In high-fraud categories, half the pins on the map are not real companies. Here is how to identify map spam accurately, report it through the channels Google monitors, and spend the rest of your effort on the visibility work that actually pays off.

Open Google Maps for a trade like locksmithing, garage door repair, or towing in a busy metro, and you will often find more listings than there are real companies. Some are keyword-stuffed versions of legitimate businesses. Others are addresses that turn out to be a parking lot, a UPS Store, or an apartment. A few are lead-generation fronts that book the job and sell it to whoever is closest. Every one of those fake pins pushes a real, licensed, insured local business down the results.

You can do something about it, but only some things, and only in some ways. This is a guide to identifying map spam accurately, reporting it through the channels Google actually monitors, setting realistic expectations for what happens next, and spending the same energy on the work that reliably moves your own ranking. It is written for the owner of a legitimate business who is tired of losing calls to listings that should not exist.

Why fighting map spam is worth your time

Local search is close to zero-sum in the map pack. Three listings show above the fold on a phone. If two of the three for "emergency locksmith near me" are fake, a real business is fighting for a single slot instead of three. Removing spam does not guarantee you move up, but it changes the math in your favor, and in high-fraud categories the effect can be large.

There is a second reason. Google's local ranking leans on prominence, which is built partly from reputation signals across the web. When fake listings collect fake reviews and fake citations, they can look prominent to Google's systems even though no real customers are behind them. Cleaning the results removes noise that the ranking model would otherwise treat as real. The mechanics behind this are covered in how local SEO works for home service businesses, and the practical priority order in local SEO ranking factors that actually matter.

The honest caveat: Google's spam enforcement is uneven and often slow. A well-documented report can sit for weeks, and some never get actioned. Treat this as ongoing hygiene, not a project with a finish line.

The economics, in plain numbers

Work it through for your own business. Say your average job is worth 400 dollars in revenue, and a typical week brings twenty calls from the map pack, of which you book twelve. If two fake listings are intercepting a quarter of the searches in your category, you might be losing five calls a week, three of which you would have booked. That is roughly 1,200 dollars a week, or a number in the tens of thousands over a year, going to listings that a licensed competitor should not be competing with. The exact figures will be different for towing, where jobs are smaller and volume is higher, or roofing, where jobs are larger and less frequent, but the shape holds: in a spammed category, the fake listings are a real line item against your revenue.

That framing also tells you how much effort is worth spending. Fifteen minutes a month is clearly worth it. Hiring a full-time person to file reports is not. The right level is a consistent routine, which the last section lays out.

The kinds of spam you will actually find

Not everything that annoys you is a reportable violation. Learn the categories so your reports are accurate.

Keyword stuffing in the business name

The most common and most reportable. A real company called "Doorworks" lists itself as "Doorworks Garage Door Repair | Same Day Service | Springs, Openers & Tracks." Google's name policy is clear: the profile name must be the real-world business name only. Anything past that, added services, cities, phone numbers, "best," "24/7," is a violation. This one is worth reporting because it is unambiguous and Google's systems can verify it against other sources.

Why it hurts you specifically: the business name is one of the strongest relevance signals Google reads. A profile named "Doorworks Garage Door Repair" is being handed the exact keyword match for "garage door repair" in the highest-weighted field on the profile. A compliant competitor named "Doorworks" has to earn that relevance through categories, services, and website content. The stuffed name is a shortcut that works until it is caught, which is why reporting it is one of the few actions with a direct competitive payoff.

Watch for the subtle versions too. "Atlanta Garage Door Repair" as a business name, when the real company is "BrightHome Services," is stuffing a city and a service into the name field. So is a trailing tagline after a real name, like "BrightHome Services - Garage Doors Done Right." The test is simple: is this the name on the truck, the invoice, the uniform, and the state registration? If not, the extra words do not belong on the profile.

Fake or unverifiable addresses

A listing shows an address that, on Street View and in reality, is a vacant lot, a residential building with no business signage, a shared mail center, or a spot several miles from where the business actually operates. Service-area businesses are supposed to hide their address; a listing that plants a visible address it cannot support is trying to appear closer to searchers than it is. This is reportable, though harder to prove without local knowledge or a photo.

Lead-generation listings

These are the trickiest. A listing looks like a local company, but the phone number routes to a call center that sells the lead, or dispatches an unvetted subcontractor. There may be a real address, real-looking reviews, and a clean name. What gives them away is a phone number that appears on dozens of listings across different cities, a website that is a thin landing page or redirects to a national brand, and an address shared with unrelated businesses. Google does act on lead-gen networks, but it usually takes a pattern across many listings, not a single report.

Lead-gen listings are worth understanding because they are the ones customers complain about most. Someone searches for a local plumber, calls the listing, and gets a dispatcher who sends whichever contractor is available and takes a cut. The customer often pays more, the work is uneven, and when something goes wrong there is no local business to hold accountable. When one of these operates in your market under a local-sounding name, it damages the trust customers have in the whole category, which is a cost even beyond the leads it takes from you. That is also why lead-gen networks are a good candidate for the fraud-reporting channels covered later, not just a Google guidelines complaint.

Duplicate profiles

One business, two or more profiles, usually at the same or nearby addresses, sometimes with slight name variations ("Metro Plumbing" and "Metro Plumbing Services"). Duplicates let a business occupy more than one map pack slot. Reporting duplicates is straightforward through the "suggest an edit" flow, and Google merges or removes them fairly reliably.

Ghost and virtual-office listings

A cluster of listings for different trades all at the same coworking space or virtual office suite. None of them have staff there. These are often part of a franchise-style lead network. Report them as not located at the stated address.

Miscategorized listings

A general handyman lists the primary category as "Plumber" and "Electrician" and "HVAC contractor" to appear for all of them. Primary category is supposed to reflect the core of the business. Additional categories have more latitude, so a handyman legitimately listing several secondary categories is not spam. A profile whose primary category is clearly wrong for what the business does is worth a report, though these are lower priority and harder to get actioned.

Fake reviews

Reviews from accounts with no photo, no history, a burst of five-star reviews for the same business on the same day, reviews that describe services the business does not offer, or reviews in the wrong language for the market. Individual fake reviews can be flagged. A clear pattern of review fraud is stronger evidence when reporting the whole profile.

Review count and rating feed prominence, and prominence is a ranking factor. A listing that buys its way to 200 five-star reviews in three months can outrank a real business with 60 honest reviews earned over five years. The pattern is usually visible if you look: reviewer accounts created the same week, one-line reviews with no specifics, no photos, and a suspicious absence of any three-star or four-star reviews, which every real business accumulates over time.

Stolen or hijacked listings

Less common but serious: a real, established business's profile gets claimed by someone else, who then redirects the phone number and website to their own operation. The reviews and history stay, which makes the listing look trustworthy, but the calls now go somewhere else. If a competitor's listing suddenly has a phone number that does not match their website and their older reviews, this may be what happened, and the real owner needs to recover it through the profile recovery process, not a spam report.

How to confirm a listing is spam before you report it

A wrong report wastes your time and Google's, and a pattern of retaliatory reporting can rebound on you. Spend ten minutes verifying before you file.

  • Check the name against other sources. Search the phone number and the address separately. Look at the company's own website, its state license lookup, and its listings on other platforms. If every other source says "Doorworks" and only Google says "Doorworks Garage Door Repair | Same Day Service," the Google name is stuffed.
  • Look at the address on Street View and a recent satellite view. Is there a building? Is there signage? Does it look like a place a plumber or roofer would operate from? Save a screenshot with the date visible.
  • Search the phone number in quotes. If it appears on listings in five other cities under different business names, it is a lead-gen number.
  • Read the website. A real local business usually has a site with a service area, real photos, a license number, and staff. A lead-gen front has a generic template, stock photos, and a form that goes nowhere identifiable.
  • Scan the reviews. Sort by newest. Look for bursts, empty profiles, and reviews that mention a different city or a service the business does not list.
  • Check for duplicates. Search the business name and the owner's name. If two profiles come up at similar addresses, note both URLs.

Write down what you found, with links and screenshots. That becomes your report.

A few tools that speed this up

  • Your state's license lookup. Most trades that require a license, plumbing, HVAC, electrical, roofing in many states, contracting, have a public database. A listing claiming licensed work with no matching license record is a strong signal, and in some cases a separate violation you can report to the licensing board.
  • A reverse phone search. Paste the listing's phone number into a search engine in quotes. Lead-gen numbers light up across cities and business names.
  • The map at different zoom levels and from different start points. Spam listings often only appear when you search from a specific area, because the operator placed the pin to intercept a particular neighborhood. Searching from three or four points across your service area surfaces listings a single search misses.
  • Street View history. If the current imagery is ambiguous, older imagery sometimes shows the location was never a business, or was a different business entirely.
  • The Wayback Machine for the listing's website. A site that was registered last month, or that used to be a parked domain, tells you the "business" is newer than its reviews suggest.

You do not need all of these for every report. Two solid pieces of evidence beat five weak ones.

The reporting channels, and which to use when

Google spreads spam reporting across several tools. Using the right one matters, because the wrong channel often means no review at all.

Suggest an edit (on the listing itself)

Best for: wrong name, wrong category, wrong hours, a business that is closed or does not exist at that location, or a duplicate. On the profile, open "Suggest an edit," then either "Change name or other details" for field corrections or "Close or remove this place" for listings that are not real. To flag a place as fraudulent, choose the option that it is not there, then select that it is offensive, harmful, or misleading, and submit. This is the fastest path for clear, single-field problems and duplicates.

Report a business on Google Maps

Best for: a listing you believe does not represent a real business, without editing a specific field. Google Maps has a "Report a business" flow that lets you explain why the listing should not appear. Use it when the whole listing is the problem rather than one detail.

The Business Redressal Complaint Form

Best for: fraudulent or misleading name, phone number, or website, and for reporting a pattern across multiple listings. This is Google's dedicated form for map spam and abuse. It lets you list multiple offending listings, describe the violation, and attach evidence. It is the strongest channel for keyword stuffing, fake addresses, and lead-gen networks, because it is built for exactly those cases and it accepts documentation.

Filling it out well takes about twenty minutes for a batch. The form asks for your details, the offending listings, and a description. For each listing, have the business name as it appears on Google, the address as listed, and the direct Maps link. In the description field, do three things: name the violation category, state what the correct information is, and point to the source that proves it. For a network, add a line explaining what ties the listings together, such as a shared phone number or an identical website template. Attach your screenshots. You will usually not get a case reference or a status update, so log the submission date and the listings yourself.

One practical note: the form is designed for reporting others, not for fixing your own listing. If your own profile has a problem, use the profile dashboard or the appeals tool instead.

Request removal of a Business Profile

Best for: a profile that clearly should not exist at all. Google Business Profile Help has a removal request path where you explain why the listing is fraudulent. After submitting, you may get follow-up questions by email, and if the request is approved the business is removed from Search and Maps.

Reporting individual reviews

Best for: specific reviews that violate policy. On each review, use the flag option and select the reason (spam, off-topic, conflict of interest). Reporting reviews one at a time is tedious and the success rate is modest, but a business owner reporting obvious fakes on a competitor's profile, with a short note, sometimes gets them removed. If you are dealing with fake reviews on your own profile, the process and the response templates are covered in getting and managing Google reviews.

What about the Help Community

The Google Business Profile Help Community has product experts who occasionally escalate well-documented spam cases, especially large lead-gen networks. It is slow and not a primary channel, but for a persistent, obvious network of fakes it is worth a factual post with the listing URLs and evidence, and no personal information.

Building a report that gets actioned

Reviewers, human or automated, act on clarity. A vague complaint gets ignored. A specific, evidenced one has a chance.

State the violation in Google's own terms

Do not write "this company is a scam." Write "the business name on this listing is 'Doorworks Garage Door Repair | Same Day Service.' The real business name, per its website and state license, is 'Doorworks.' The listing name contains service keywords, which violates the business name guideline."

Give the evidence

  • The exact listing URL, or the business name plus address so the reviewer can find it.
  • A link to the business's real name on its website or a license lookup.
  • A dated screenshot of Street View for address claims.
  • The phone number and a note of where else it appears, for lead-gen cases.
  • For duplicates, both listing URLs and a one-line note that they are the same business.

Report the pattern, not just one pin

If a lead-gen network has fifteen listings across your metro, report them together in the Redressal Form with all fifteen URLs and one clear description. A single listing looks like an outlier. Fifteen with the same phone number and website template looks like what it is.

Keep records

Log the date, the channel, what you reported, and any response. If nothing happens in a month, you can re-file with the same evidence through a different channel, or take it to the Help Community with a record of what you have already tried.

What Google's spam systems can and cannot see

Understanding how enforcement works keeps your expectations realistic and your reports useful.

What the systems catch on their own

Google runs automated detection that compares a listing's data against the rest of the web. It is reasonably good at spotting a business name that does not match any other source, a phone number that appears on hundreds of listings, a website that redirects to a national aggregator, and review patterns that look purchased. Machine-verifiable violations like keyword stuffing are the ones most likely to be actioned without a report at all, eventually.

What needs a human, and therefore a good report

Some things a system cannot judge from data alone. Whether a small office building actually houses a plumbing dispatch operation, whether a listing at a strip mall is a real storefront or a rented mailbox, whether a category is genuinely wrong for the work a business does. These need local knowledge, and that is what your report provides. A dated photo, a note that you drove past and there is no signage, a link to the real business under a different name: these move a case a system could not resolve.

Why enforcement is slow and uneven

Google processes an enormous volume of listings and reports. Clear, high-confidence violations get handled faster. Ambiguous ones queue. Lead-gen networks are often removed in coordinated sweeps rather than one listing at a time, so a report you filed in March might contribute to a takedown in June. None of this is visible to you. The practical response is to file good reports, log them, and check back rather than expecting a reply.

The limits of what removal achieves

Removing a fake listing does not automatically promote you into its old position. The map pack recalculates based on relevance, distance, and prominence for each search. Clearing spam removes competition and noise, which helps, but if your own profile is weak, another listing, fake or real, may take the spot. That is why the two halves of this work, reporting spam and strengthening your own presence, belong together.

What outcomes are realistic

Set expectations so you are not chasing a result that rarely comes.

  • Keyword-stuffed names get corrected reasonably often, because the violation is clear and machine-verifiable. Expect weeks, not days, and expect some to be ignored.
  • Duplicates are the most reliable win. Google merges these fairly consistently.
  • Fake addresses are hit or miss. A dated photo showing no business at the location helps a lot. Without one, these often stall.
  • Lead-gen networks get taken down in waves, usually after enough reports and internal detection line up. Your report contributes to that, but one owner rarely triggers it alone.
  • Category and review reports have the lowest success rates. Do them, but do not count on them.

You will not get a case number or a status page for most of these. Sometimes you notice weeks later that a listing changed or vanished. That is the normal outcome for a successful report.

Avoiding false reports and retaliation wars

It is tempting to report every competitor who outranks you. Do not.

Reporting a legitimate business out of frustration is both unethical and risky. Google's systems track reporting behavior, and a pattern of reports that turn out to be baseless can reduce the weight Google gives to your future reports, or worse, draw scrutiny to your own profile. If a competitor is ranking above you with a clean listing, a real address, and real reviews, they are not spam. They are just doing local SEO well, and the answer is to do it better, not to try to get them removed.

Retaliation cycles are the other trap. You report a competitor, they report you, and now both profiles are under review and both businesses lose visibility while Google sorts it out. If your own listing is fully compliant this usually resolves in your favor, but it costs weeks, and if the retaliation gets your profile suspended you are into the reinstatement process. Keep your profile clean enough that a retaliatory report goes nowhere, which the Google Business Profile checklist lays out, and only report what is genuinely, verifiably a violation.

A simple test before you file: could you explain this report to the competitor's face and defend it? If yes, file it. If it is really "they rank above me and I am annoyed," skip it.

When map spam is fraud you should report elsewhere too

Some map spam is not just a Google guidelines problem. It is unlicensed contracting, deceptive advertising, or outright consumer fraud. In those cases, Google is one channel, not the only one.

  • State licensing board. A listing advertising licensed trade work, electrical, plumbing, HVAC, roofing, without a valid license is a matter for the board that issues those licenses. Boards can and do act on complaints, and an enforcement action creates a public record that also helps a Google report.
  • State attorney general or consumer protection office. Deceptive practices, fake addresses used to appear local, bait-and-switch pricing, and unvetted subcontractors sent under a fake brand can all be consumer protection violations.
  • The Federal Trade Commission. The FTC takes reports on deceptive advertising and has pursued lead-generation networks in the home services space. A single report does not trigger action, but volume does, and reporting is quick.
  • Your local trade association or contractor group. Many have processes for documenting fraudulent operators, and a coordinated set of complaints from real local businesses carries more weight than one.

This is not the first move for a keyword-stuffed name. It is the right move when the "business" is actively defrauding customers in your market and hurting the reputation of the trade. Keep your documentation clean either way, because the same evidence supports all of these channels.

The better use of the same energy: your own visibility

Every hour spent reporting spam is an hour not spent on the things that reliably raise your own ranking. In most markets, the second category is the better investment.

Make your listing unambiguously the real thing

  • Exact legal name, no keywords. A clean name is not just compliance, it is a trust signal.
  • Correct primary category, with honest secondary categories.
  • Complete services list, real photos added monthly, hours that are actually right including holidays.
  • A steady flow of reviews from real customers, with a reply to every one.

Fix the data Google cross-checks

Google verifies your listing against other sources on the web. If your name, address, and phone are inconsistent across your website, your listings on other platforms, and industry directories, Google trusts the listing less. Auditing and fixing that is one of the highest-value, least-glamorous local SEO tasks, and the process is in the guide to NAP consistency.

Build genuine local prominence

Real mentions from local news, suppliers, associations, and community organizations tell Google your business is established in the area. This is the signal fake listings cannot fake for long, and it is what pulls a real business up as spam gets cleared out.

Strengthen the website behind the profile

The listing sends traffic; the website converts it and reinforces relevance. A site with clear service and service-area pages, real proof of work, and fast load times supports the profile's ranking and captures the searches that do not start on Google.

Why real businesses win as spam clears out

Fake listings have a ceiling. They can stuff a name, plant an address, and buy reviews, but they cannot build the slower signals that Google increasingly weights: consistent data across dozens of legitimate sources, genuine mentions from local organizations, a review history that shows the natural spread of a real customer base over years, photos that accumulate month after month, and a website with real depth. A lead-gen front is built to be cheap and disposable, because it expects to be caught. Your business is built to last. When enforcement clears a batch of fakes, the listings that move up are the ones with those durable signals, and that is the position you want to be in before the cleanup happens, not after.

Put differently: reporting spam is defense, and building prominence is offense. Teams that only play defense spend all their time filing reports and never actually pull ahead. Teams that only play offense keep losing slots to fakes that a ten-minute report would have removed. The businesses that dominate a spammed category do both, on a schedule, without letting either one become a distraction from running the company.

Cleaning up a locksmith map, step by step

Locksmithing is the category where map spam is worst, so it is a useful example. Here is how a real, licensed locksmith worked through it over a few months.

The owner searched "locksmith near me" and "emergency locksmith" from three points across the metro. The map pack and the top Maps results held twelve listings. The owner recognized four as real local competitors. The other eight needed checking.

Of the eight: three shared a single 800-style phone number and had websites that were near-identical single-page templates with stock photos of keys. Two listed addresses that, on Street View, were a residential apartment complex and a vacant retail unit. One had a business name of "24 Hour Emergency Locksmith Service" with no other identifying information. Two had a plausible name and address but a burst of nineteen five-star reviews all posted within a four-day window in the prior month, from accounts with no other activity.

The owner built one Redressal Form submission listing all eight, grouped: the three network listings with a note about the shared phone and template, the two fake addresses with dated Street View screenshots, the keyword-name listing, and the two with review bursts described with the dates. Total time: about forty minutes including the verification.

Results over the following ten weeks: the keyword-name listing had its name changed to something generic and then disappeared. Two of the three network listings were removed. The two fake-address listings were unchanged. The review-burst listings kept their reviews but stopped gaining new ones. The owner re-filed the two unactioned fake-address listings through the Maps "report a business" flow and one was removed a month later.

Net effect: the real map pack for the owner's core terms went from "mostly fakes" to "mostly real businesses," and the owner's own listing, which had also gained forty legitimate reviews and a full photo set during the same period, moved from the edge of the map pack into it consistently. Neither change alone would have done it.

A monthly competitive-hygiene routine

Fifteen minutes a month keeps this manageable and keeps you from either ignoring the problem or obsessing over it.

  • Search your two or three core "service + near me" terms from a location central to your service area. Note the map pack and the top ten Maps results.
  • For any listing you do not recognize, run the ten-minute verification check.
  • Report the clear violations through the right channel, with evidence. Log it.
  • Check last month's reports for any that got actioned. Update your log.
  • Spend the remaining time on one improvement to your own profile or site: a batch of new photos, three review requests, one fixed citation, one new local page.

Over a year that adds up to a cleaner local map in your category and a materially stronger profile of your own. If your market is heavily spammed and the map pack is genuinely broken, it is worth having someone audit both the competitive picture and your local SEO setup, because in those categories the businesses that win are the ones treating this as a routine, not a one-time cleanup.

One more habit worth building: keep a simple shared document with your team. When a technician mentions that a customer said they "called the other 24-hour place first and got quoted double," that is field intelligence about a lead-gen operator in your area. When your office manager notices the same wrong number listed for your business on a directory, that is a citation problem worth fixing. The people answering your phones and driving your trucks see the local market every day, and a place to log what they notice turns scattered observations into a monthly cleanup list.

The goal is not a perfectly clean map, which you will not get. The goal is to stop treating map spam as either invisible or infuriating, and start treating it as a normal cost of doing business in a competitive local category, one you manage in fifteen minutes a month while you put the real work into being the business Google and customers both recognize as legitimate.

Common Questions

Reporting questions, answered.

Will Google tell the other business that I reported them?
No. Reports are not attributed to the reporter, and the business being reported is not told who filed. They may receive a notification that their listing is under review or that an edit was suggested, but not by whom.
How many listings can I report at once?
Through the Business Redressal Complaint Form you can list multiple offending listings in a single submission, which is the right approach for a lead-generation network. Through "suggest an edit" you report one listing at a time.
A competitor keeps keyword-stuffing their name after every correction. What can I do?
Re-report it each time through the Redressal Form, and keep a log showing the repeated pattern. A profile that repeatedly violates the name policy after corrections is more likely to draw a stronger action, including suspension, than one that does it once.
Is it against the rules to report a competitor?
No, as long as the report is accurate. Google provides these tools for exactly this purpose. What crosses the line is filing reports you know to be false, or a pattern of baseless reports, which can reduce the weight of your future reports.
One of my own real listings got removed after a competitor reported it. How do I fix it?
File a reinstatement appeal through the Google Business Profile appeals tool with documents proving the business is real and operating. The full process is in our guide on recovering a suspended Google Business Profile.
Should I use a paid service that promises to clean up my local map?
Be cautious. These services mostly file the same reports you can file yourself, and none can guarantee removals. If a market is badly spammed, the value is in someone doing the monthly routine consistently and improving your own listing at the same time, not in a one-time cleanup package.
