Local SEO Checklist for 2027: What to Review and Fix Before January

A practical December-to-January checklist for local and home service businesses: capture your 2026 numbers, fix what decayed over the year, check how AI tools describe you, and walk into 2027 with goals and a budget.

Quick answer: A year-end local SEO review has four jobs. First, save your 2026 numbers (calls, direction requests, reviews, Search Console clicks, booked jobs) so you have a baseline. Second, fix the basics that decay over a year: Google Business Profile details, hours, reviews, name-address-phone consistency, and stale website content. Third, check how your business shows up in AI answers. Fourth, set three measurable goals and a budget for 2027. Do the first two in December, while many businesses slow down, and the last two in the first week of January. The full checklist is at the bottom of this page.

Most local businesses never review their online presence on purpose. A profile gets set up, a website goes live, and then the details drift. A phone number changes, a service gets added to the van but not to the profile, a competitor collects forty reviews, a holiday banner stays up until March. None of it is dramatic. It adds up to a slow loss of calls that nobody notices, because nobody compared this year with last year.

The end of the year is the best time to do that comparison. Your numbers for 2026 are complete, many trades are in a quieter stretch, and you can enter January with a plan instead of habits. This guide is a practical checklist for a home service or local business. It covers what to measure, what to fix, what to check for AI search, and how to set goals and a budget that you can actually track. It does not promise rankings or invent benchmarks. Where a number matters, you will find where to look it up for your own business.

If you only have an hour, take our free Local SEO Audit Quiz first. It points to the areas below where your business is weakest, so you know which sections to read first.

Why a year-end review is worth doing in December

There are four practical reasons to do this now rather than in the spring.

  • The data is complete. A full calendar year of calls, reviews, and search data shows seasonality that a quarter hides. You can see which months brought calls and which did not, and plan around them.
  • Some data does not last. Google Search Console keeps about 16 months of data, so a comparison with last winter disappears if you wait too long. Other dashboards also limit how far back they go. Save a copy of what you need now.
  • December is often quieter for non-emergency work. Marketing fixes take hours, not weeks, but they need someone's attention. If your busy season is spring or summer, the next eight weeks are the cheapest time to do them.
  • January is when people decide. Searches for providers, plan upgrades, and quotes tend to pick up after the holidays. A profile and website that are correct on January 2 capture that interest. One that is fixed in March misses it.

This does not replace regular maintenance. If you already follow a quarterly SEO audit checklist, treat this as the larger annual version: the same checks, plus a look back at the year and a plan for the next one.

You do not need to do everything at once. This order keeps the work small and puts the highest-value steps first.

Step 1: Capture your 2026 baseline

Before you change anything, write down where you are. A baseline turns "I think it helped" into "calls from the profile went from this to that." Use a simple spreadsheet with one row per metric and one column per month, and save screenshots as backup.

If you do not track booked jobs by source, start now. Even a tally on paper beats guessing. Our guide to tracking local SEO ROI explains a simple way to do it, and the free ROI Calculator helps you turn call and job numbers into a return figure.

Two cautions. First, do not compare raw December numbers with the summer. Seasonal trades swing a lot, so compare each month with the same month last year where you can. Second, a metric that fell is a clue, not a verdict. A drop in website clicks can mean fewer searches, a ranking change, or a tracking problem. Note it, then check the cause before reacting.

Step 2: Check your Google Business Profile

For most local businesses the profile is the most valuable page they own, and it is also the one that decays fastest. Go through it line by line. Our full Google Business Profile checklist has the details, and this is the year-end short list.

  1. Primary category. Is it still the best description of the work that earns the most? Google adds and renames categories, so check the current list. The GBP Category Finder helps you search it, and our guide to choosing categories explains how to decide.
  2. Secondary categories. Keep only categories for services you really offer. Remove anything you no longer do.
  3. Services and products. Add the jobs you now do and remove the ones you stopped. See how to use the services section.
  4. Business description. Does it name what you do, where, and what makes you different, in plain language? Check that every claim is still true.
  5. Hours. Confirm regular hours and set special hours for holiday dates. Our guide to profile hours lists the 2026 to 2027 dates.
  6. Service area or address. If you serve customers at their location, make sure the service area matches where you really work. Read the guide on service area businesses if your setup has changed.
  7. Photos. Replace old or generic pictures with recent photos of your team, vehicles, and completed jobs. A photo gallery that is two years old looks inactive.
  8. Posts and updates. If the last post is from last spring, plan a simple rhythm of short updates for 2027.
  9. Contact details and links. Check the phone number, website link, and any booking or quote link. Make sure they all work.
  10. Edits and suggestions. Look for changes made by others and review pending edits. A wrong phone number from a customer suggestion is more common than most owners expect.
  11. Duplicates and access. Search for duplicate profiles for your business and check that only the right people have access.

If anything looks wrong, such as a profile that is not showing up or a warning about verification, fix that first, because nothing else matters while the profile is unavailable. These two guides cover the common problems: why a profile is not showing up and what to do about a suspension.

Step 3: Look at your reviews

Reviews are one of the strongest trust signals for local customers, and they are one of the few things you cannot fix in a single afternoon. That is why the year-end review should include a plain look at where you stand and a process for next year.

Check these five things:

  • Total count and average rating, compared with the two or three businesses that rank above you in your area. You are not trying to match them overnight. You are trying to see how far there is to go.
  • Recency. How many reviews did you collect in the last 90 days? A steady trickle looks healthier than a burst followed by silence.
  • Response rate. Have you replied to every review, good and bad? Replies show future customers how you treat people. See how to respond to Google reviews.
  • What customers actually say. Read the last 30 reviews and note repeated words. They tell you which services and qualities customers value, and they often show a problem to fix.
  • The ask. Does every finished job end with a review request? If it depends on someone remembering, it will not happen consistently.

The fix is a routine, not a campaign. Ask every customer for an honest review at the end of the job, send a direct review link by text, and never offer rewards for reviews, which breaks Google's policies. Step 4: Check your name, address, and phone everywhere

Your business name, address, and phone number (NAP) should read the same on your website, your profile, and the directories that list you. Over a year, small differences creep in: an old suite number, a tracking number, "LLC" on one listing and not on another. Search engines and AI tools compare these details to decide how much to trust you.

  1. Write down your exact NAP as you want it to appear everywhere.
  2. Search for your business by name, by phone number, and by any old address, and note every listing that appears.
  3. Compare each listing with your master version and fix the differences, starting with the core platforms and the biggest directories.
  4. Report or merge duplicates where a site offers a way to do so.
  5. Record what you changed so the next review starts from a clean list.

The Local Citation Sites Finder gives you a country-by-country list of directories to check, with a progress tracker. For the method, see NAP consistency and local rankings, what local citations are, and our citation cleanup guide.

Step 5: Review your website

A website that was fine a year ago may now be slow, out of date, or missing the pages that customers search for. You do not need a redesign. You need a short list of fixes.

Content that has gone stale

Remove or update anything with a date on it that has passed: holiday banners, expired offers, old team photos, "2025" or "2026" in headings, and a copyright year in the footer. Set a reminder to change the footer year on January 1. Small details like these tell a visitor whether the business is still active.

Service and location pages

List the services you want more calls for and the places you serve. Does each one have a page that answers what a customer wants to know: what you do, where, how it works, what it costs or how pricing works, and how to book? Pages that are thin or copied from each other rarely help. If you serve many towns, read the guide on service area pages vs city pages before you create more.

Titles, headings, and internal links

Check that your most important pages have a clear title and heading that name the service and the place in natural language. Link related pages to each other, such as a service page to the locations it covers, so visitors and search engines can follow the path.

Speed and mobile

Most local searches happen on phones. Open your own site on a phone over mobile data and try to call, book, or request a quote. If it is slow or awkward, fix it before anything else. Our guides to website speed and local rankings and a mobile optimization checklist list the common causes.

Conversion

Traffic that does not call is wasted. Check that the phone number is clickable, the form is short, and every page has an obvious next step. Read how to turn visitors into booked jobs if your numbers show plenty of visits but few leads. Our list of website design mistakes is a useful second check.

Structured data

Structured data tells search engines the facts about your business in a standard format: name, address, phone, hours, and services. If you have none, or it is out of date, update it and make sure it matches your profile. Our free Local Business Schema Generator creates the markup, and this guide to LocalBusiness schema explains where it goes.

Step 6: Refresh the content that already works

New content is not always the best use of time. Start with the pages and posts that already get visits or impressions. Open Search Console, sort pages by impressions, and look at the top ten. For each one, ask:

  • Is every fact on it still true, including prices, phone numbers, and service areas?
  • Does it answer the question people searched for, clearly, near the top?
  • Does it link to your relevant service page and to related guides?
  • Does it end with a clear next step?

Then plan your 2027 content around real demand. Local keyword research shows how to find the questions customers ask, and our seasonal marketing calendar helps you time each topic. A short, honest list of six to twelve topics for the year is better than a plan you will not keep.

Step 7: Check your links and local mentions

Links from other local sites are a signal that your business is real and known in its community. You do not need a campaign to improve this. Start with a list of what you already have: suppliers, manufacturers, chambers of commerce, local associations, sponsored teams, and any press. Then check that each one links to the right page and shows the right details.

Next, list five or ten real opportunities for 2027, such as a local sponsorship, a supplier directory, a community event, or a partnership with a related trade. Avoid buying links or joining link schemes, which put the whole site at risk. Our guide to local link building for home service businesses explains what works and what to avoid.

Step 8: Check how AI search describes your business

More customers now ask an AI tool or an assistant for a recommendation instead of scrolling a list of links. Those tools draw on your website, your profile, and the listings and reviews they can find. The goal for 2027 is simple: make sure the facts they find are correct and consistent.

  1. Ask the questions your customers ask. Try a few searches such as "best [your trade] in [your town]" and "who does [your service] near me" in the AI tools you have access to, and note whether your business appears and how it is described.
  2. Look for wrong facts. Old hours, a service you no longer offer, or the wrong service area usually trace back to an outdated listing or page. Fix the source.
  3. Make the facts easy to find. State what you do, where you work, and how to reach you in plain text on your site, and keep structured data and your profile consistent with it.
  4. Keep the foundations strong. Reviews, consistent listings, and clear service pages help both classic and AI results.

Nobody outside the AI companies can say exactly how these tools choose what to show, so be wary of anyone promising a guaranteed result. What you can do is remove errors and make your facts clear. Read more in how to get cited by AI search, structured data for AI search, how to measure AI search visibility, and what GEO is.

Step 9: Make sure tracking works

A goal you cannot measure is a hope. Before January, test the tools that will give you your 2027 numbers.

  • Phone calls. Can you tell which calls came from Google, your website, or elsewhere? Call tracking or a simple tally works. If you use tracking numbers, keep your main number consistent on your profile and listings.
  • Forms and bookings. Submit a test form and confirm that it reaches the right inbox and is counted in your analytics.
  • Search Console and analytics. Check that both are installed, that you can see the data, and that the right people have access.
  • A monthly reminder. Put a 30-minute slot in the calendar on the same day each month to record your numbers.

If you have never had a regular review habit, our quarterly SEO audit checklist turns this into a repeatable routine.

Step 10: Set three goals for 2027

Pick three goals and no more. Each should be a number you can measure, tied to a result that matters to the business, with a date. Here are examples of good shapes for a goal. The numbers are placeholders for you to replace with your own, based on your baseline:

Our guide on how long local SEO takes explains what is reasonable to expect and why. It is better to set a goal you can beat than one that needs a miracle.

Step 11: Plan your 2027 budget

A local marketing budget is a decision about where the next dollar earns the most. Use your baseline to make it. The usual choices fall into four groups:

  • Foundations: profile management, citations and listings, review collection, and tracking. These are low cost and underpin everything else.
  • Website work: fixing speed, building or improving service and location pages, and structured data.
  • Content and links: a small, steady program of useful pages and local links.
  • Paid: ads or Local Services Ads for the services where speed matters, if your phone can handle the calls. See SEO vs paid ads and our guide to Local Services Ads for home services.

Start with the group where your baseline shows the biggest gap. Our guide to how much to spend on marketing for a local business explains how to set a number, and the Local SEO Cost Calculator shows what common services cost for one to three locations. Write down not just the total but what you will stop doing if a channel does not work after a fair trial.

If you have more than one location

A multi-location business has one extra job: keeping every location's profile, page, and listings consistent while still showing local detail. Give each location its own profile and page with unique, accurate information, use one master document for names, hours, and services, and review each location's numbers separately, because an average can hide a weak location. Our guide to multi-location local SEO and the article on how many locations to list online cover the details.

Ten year-end review mistakes

  1. Skipping the baseline. Without it you cannot show that anything worked.
  2. Fixing everything at once. Changing profile, site, and listings in the same week makes results impossible to read. Spread the work out and note dates.
  3. Chasing rankings instead of leads. A top position that does not produce calls is not the goal.
  4. Setting goals with no number. "Get more reviews" is not a goal. "Collect 30 reviews by March 31" is.
  5. Leaving seasonal content up. Old holiday banners and expired offers make a site look neglected.
  6. Ignoring the phone. Marketing that brings calls to a phone nobody answers wastes the spend.
  7. Trusting a single dashboard. Cross-check profile data, Search Console, and your own job records.
  8. Buying shortcuts. Paid reviews, link schemes, and fake locations can get a profile suspended.
  9. Forgetting AI search. Customers increasingly ask assistants, and wrong facts there cost real calls.
  10. Not scheduling the next review. A review with no follow-up date is a one-off. Put the quarterly check in the calendar before you finish.

The 2027 local SEO checklist

  • 2026 baseline saved: profile calls and clicks, Search Console, analytics, booked jobs, reviews, and rankings.
  • Primary and secondary categories checked against the current category list.
  • Services, description, photos, and links on the profile are accurate and recent.
  • Regular and holiday hours are correct, and special hours are set for the holidays.
  • Reviews: every review has a reply, and every finished job gets a review request.
  • NAP is consistent across the website, profile, and main directories.
  • Duplicate and wrong listings are reported, merged, or removed.
  • Old dates, banners, and offers are removed from the website, and the footer year is set to change in January.
  • Key service and location pages exist, are useful, and link to each other.
  • The site is fast and easy to use on a phone, with a clickable phone number and a short form.
  • Structured data is present and matches your profile.
  • Top pages by impressions have been refreshed.
  • Local links and mentions are listed, with five to ten opportunities for 2027.
  • AI answers checked for wrong facts, and the sources fixed.
  • Call, form, and analytics tracking has been tested end to end.
  • Three measurable goals are written down, with dates.
  • A 2027 budget is set, with a note on what you will stop if a channel does not work.
  • A monthly numbers check and a quarterly audit are in the calendar.

Common Questions

Frequently asked.

When should I do a year-end local SEO review?
December is a good time, because your numbers for the year are complete and many businesses are in a quieter stretch. Save your baseline early in the month, fix the basics in the second half, and set goals and a budget in the first week of January.
What should a local SEO checklist include?
A solid checklist covers your Google Business Profile, reviews, name-address-phone consistency across listings, your website pages and speed, structured data, content, links, tracking, and how AI tools describe your business. It should end with measurable goals and a budget.
What numbers should I save before the year ends?
Save profile calls, direction requests, website clicks, and messages, Search Console clicks and impressions, organic visits and conversions, booked jobs by source, review count and rating, and your rankings for key searches. Search Console only keeps about 16 months, so capture it now.
How often should I update my Google Business Profile?
Review the details at least once a quarter, and check hours before every holiday. Add photos and short updates regularly. A full line-by-line review once a year, like the one in this guide, catches the slow drift that quarterly checks miss.
Do I need to change my website every year for local SEO?
No, but you do need to keep it accurate. Update anything dated, such as banners, offers, and the footer year, fix pages that are slow or hard to use on a phone, and refresh the pages that already get impressions. A full redesign is rarely needed.
How do I check whether AI tools describe my business correctly?
Search for your trade and town in the AI tools and assistants customers use, and see whether you appear and how you are described. If the hours, services, or area are wrong, find the outdated listing or page the answer came from and correct it. Consistent facts help more than any trick.
How many goals should I set for local SEO?
Three is plenty. Choose goals you can measure, such as calls from Google, new reviews, or booked jobs from online sources, and give each one a date. Too many goals spread attention thin and make it hard to see what worked.
How much should I budget for local SEO in 2027?
It depends on your market, your competition, and where your baseline shows the biggest gap. Start with low-cost foundations such as profile management, listings, and reviews, then add website and content work, and use paid ads only where your phone can handle the calls. Decide in advance what you will stop if something does not work.
